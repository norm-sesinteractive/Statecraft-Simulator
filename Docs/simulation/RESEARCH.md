# Research grounding

> **Owns:** the real-world findings each mechanic leans on, and where the game departs from them (named
> concessions). **Budget:** 400 lines. **Read when:** justifying, questioning or tuning a mechanic.

Norm's projects keep at least one leg in realism. This doc maps mechanics to the social science behind them.
Citations come from Claude's knowledge (2026-10-14) and have not been independently checked; verify one before
quoting it in game text. Treat findings as directions, never as tuning values: the game is a caricature that
amplifies effects that are small and contested in life, and each such amplification is named below.

## The seven vices as psychology

- The seven deadly sins are a moral list, not a psychological one: from Evagrius Ponticus's eight evil thoughts
  (4th century) to Gregory the Great's seven (6th century).
- Readings used here: Wrath as aggression and hostility; Greed as materialism; Lust as mating effort and
  hedonism; Pride as individualism, status seeking and narcissism; Envy as upward social comparison (Festinger
  1954) and relative deprivation; Gluttony as consumption and indulgence; Sloth as low conscientiousness,
  apathy and acceptance.

> Concession: treating seven moral categories as measurable drives of a whole society is a game abstraction.

## Media: fast mood, slow culture

- Agenda setting: media decide what people think about (McCombs and Shaw 1972).
- Cultivation theory: long, heavy exposure shapes people's picture of the world, as in "mean world syndrome"
  (Gerbner and Gross 1976). Basis for the slow drift of disposition.
- Media violence and aggression: short-term priming and long-term scripts in the General Aggression Model
  (Anderson and Bushman 2002); meta-analyses report small effects (Anderson et al. 2010), and the size is
  disputed (Ferguson and colleagues). Basis for Wrath airtime raising expressed Wrath.
- Social learning: children imitate modeled aggression (Bandura, Ross and Ross 1961). Basis for youth being the
  most susceptible cohort.
- Manufactured consent: Lippmann, Public Opinion (1922); Bernays, Propaganda (1928); Herman and Chomsky,
  Manufacturing Consent (1988). The hidden hand's tradition.

> Concession: real media effects are small and slow. The game makes them strong enough to steer by.

## Education and generations

- Values change mostly by generational replacement, not by people changing their minds (Inglehart 1977, The
  Silent Revolution). Basis for the cohort model and for schooling's lag.
- Formative years: attitudes crystallize in late adolescence and early adulthood (Mannheim 1928, "The Problem
  of Generations"; Krosnick and Alwin 1989, the impressionable-years hypothesis). Basis for schools training youth.

> Concession: "channeling", trained habits that give a drive an outlet, is a game construct inspired by
> sublimation and social roles. It is not a measured quantity.

## Outlets, satisfaction and frustration

- Frustration-aggression: blocked goals breed aggression (Dollard et al. 1939; reformulated by Berkowitz 1989).
  Basis for frustration feeding crime and unrest.
- Catharsis is not supported: venting anger increases aggression (Bushman 2002).

> Concession: outlets here are socially sanctioned roles (soldier, merchant, artist, scholar) that give a drive
> a respected place, closer to sublimation and institutional roles than to venting. The image is a warrior
> culture that honors its warriors, not a population letting off steam.

## Crime and the courts

- Deterrence: the certainty of punishment deters more than its severity (Beccaria 1764; Nagin 2013 review).
  Crime as a choice under expected punishment (Becker 1968).
- Legitimacy: people obey laws they see as fair (Tyler 1990, Why People Obey the Law). Harsh or unfair
  enforcement erodes that.
- Strain: crime rises when valued goals lack legitimate means (Merton 1938). Basis for frustrated Greed feeding crime.

> Concession: v0 has one severity dial with linear deterrence. Certainty, legitimacy and the diminishing
> returns of severity are [PLANNED].

## Contentment

- Needs come in layers, basic before belonging and esteem (Maslow 1943).
- Happiness adapts: the Easterlin paradox (Easterlin 1974) and the hedonic treadmill (Brickman and Campbell
  1971). [PLANNED: expectations that rise with prosperity.]

## Unrest and revolution

- Relative deprivation: people rebel over the gap between what they expect and what they get (Gurr 1970, Why
  Men Rebel). Basis for Envy amplifying unrest.
- The J-curve: revolutions follow a sharp reversal after a period of rising expectations (Davies 1962).
  [PLANNED: expectations.]
- Structural-demographic theory: popular immiseration, elite overproduction and state fiscal strain drive
  cycles of instability (Goldstone 1991; Turchin and Nefedov 2009, Secular Cycles).

## Rise and decadence

- Asabiyyah: hardy, cohesive peoples conquer soft, settled ones, then soften themselves within a few
  generations (Ibn Khaldun, Muqaddimah, 1377). This is Norm's question about happy peoples growing "weak and
  decadent" and being overthrown, stated six centuries earlier.
- Glubb, The Fate of Empires (1976): an age of pioneers, then conquest, commerce, affluence, intellect and
  decadence. Popular rather than scholarly; useful as a narrative template.
- Olson, The Rise and Decline of Nations (1982): stable societies accumulate interest groups that slow them.
- "Bread and circuses" (Juvenal, Satire X): a fed and entertained people stops reaching for power. Basis for
  Sloth and Gluttony as stabilizers.

## War

- Arms races as coupled dynamics (Richardson 1960, Arms and Insecurity).
- Wars begin when nations disagree about their relative strength (Blainey 1973, The Causes of War); the
  rationalist account adds private information and commitment problems (Fearon 1995).
- The security dilemma: one side's defense looks like the other's threat (Herz 1950; Jervis 1978).

## Trade and peace

- Commerce softens manners and makes war costly (Montesquieu 1748); the commercial and democratic peace
  (Kant 1795; Oneal and Russett 1999).
- Trade grows with economic size and shrinks with distance: the gravity model (Tinbergen 1962).

## Culture

- Soft power: attraction rather than coercion (Nye 1990).
- Neighbors' cultures converge through interaction (Axelrod 1997).
- Art as display (Miller 2000, The Mating Mind): a playful anchor for Lust driving cultural output.

## Individualism, innovation and cohesion

- Individualism and collectivism as a cultural dimension (Hofstede 1980). Individualist cultures innovate more
  (Gorodnichenko and Roland 2017), which matches Norm's Pride: "high highs in technological growth, but also
  drives people apart".
- Social capital erodes as people disengage from each other (Putnam 2000, Bowling Alone).

## Demography

- The demographic transition: fertility falls as societies grow richer and more individualist (Notestein 1945).
- Malthusian pressure: population outruns food without new technology (Malthus 1798). Basis for Gluttony and
  scarcity pushing nations toward their neighbors' land.
